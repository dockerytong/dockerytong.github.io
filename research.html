Pyrite oxidation when oxygen is not the acceptor

Question
Under circumnatural, anoxic conditions pyrite still oxidizes, and the rate is fast enough to support autotrophic denitrification. What actually sets that rate?
Finding
The answer is not oxygen supply but the electron budget: raising the redox potential of the solution accelerates pyrite oxidation under neutral pH, and the surface either keeps transferring electrons or passivates depending on how the potential is held. J. Hazard. Mater. 2021; Electrochim. Acta 2021.
Lever
If the rate is set by the electron budget, it should respond to an applied potential rather than to dosing. In-operando electrochemical stimulation of pyrite autotrophic denitrification confirms that it does. Nitrogen Cycling 2026.
Mechanism
Passivation is not a fixed property of the surface: cysteine adsorption weakens the Fe–S lattice and lets oxidation proceed, so organic coverage can switch the interface between passivated and active. Appl. Surf. Sci. 2025.

Mineral photocatalysis and iron cycling in the euphotic zone

Question
Seawater carries semiconductor mineral particles and dissolved iron that microbes need. Light is the largest periodic input to that zone — does it set the Fe(II)/Fe(III) ratio?
Finding
Mineral photocatalysis sustains Fe(II)/Fe(III) cycling in the euphotic zone, which makes light a control input on iron speciation rather than a background condition. Nat. Commun. 2026.
Why it matters
Iron speciation is normally treated as a consequence of redox chemistry and biology. If photocatalysis sets it, then the same interface is doing the converting, and the daily light cycle is an input that can be counted rather than averaged away.

The reductive side of pyrite

Question
Pyrite oxidation is well mapped. What happens to the same mineral under reducing conditions, and is the transformation path symmetric?
Observation
In situ Raman indicates that pyrite transforms readily under reducing conditions while oxidation is comparatively difficult — the opposite of the usual expectation.
Status
Mechanism under study, with DFT on the reductive pathway.

Tailings to soil: aggregates, metals, and organic matter

Question
Tailings spontaneously develop soil-like structure. Which interfacial reactions do the cementing, and can they be steered?
Approach
Paired long-term incubation and field samples, with structure (aggregate stability, µCT), element speciation (XAS, XPS), and electrochemistry on the same sample IDs, so that a change in mineral phase can be read against a change in structure and in metal mobility.
Status
Current group programme. The computational part is mine: interface energetics and phase transformation pathways at the atomistic level, plus machine-learned potentials where they are trustworthy and not where they are not.

Methods

Electrochemistry is the core: cyclic voltammetry, impedance (including dynamic impedance during reaction), chronoamperometry, and in-operando cells that follow a mineral surface while it reacts. Structure and speciation come from Raman and infrared spectroscopy, XPS, and synchrotron XAS; the atomistic side is DFT (adsorption, lattice response, electron transfer paths) with machine-learned interatomic potentials used for sampling and screening. Microbial community composition is read from amplicon and metagenomic data when the question is who is consuming the electrons.

On machine-learned potentials: I use them for ranking and trends, not for absolute energetics at metal–oxide interfaces, and I check the geometry they produce before believing an energy. Details in Notes.
